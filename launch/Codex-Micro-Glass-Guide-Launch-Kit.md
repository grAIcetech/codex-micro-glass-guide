## Article body

I have a very cute—and not inexpensive—device on my desk: the Work Louder × OpenAI Codex Micro.

The Codex Micro marked the first limited release of a functional physical Codex product. The demonstration videos made it look almost magical. People moved through their work with a dial, joystick, glowing Agent keys, and six programmable layers.

I thought: yes. I am going to use this for everything.

Then I sat down in front of mine and forgot what the buttons did.

That is the problem with programmable hardware. Seeing what it can do is not the same as remembering how to do it. One button can mean something completely different on another layer. If I have to stop, search the documentation, and reconstruct the map in my head every time, the friction wins.

And I did not buy a lovely, expensive tool so it could become a dust-gathering paperweight.

## The missing bridge

The hardware was not the problem. The awkward learning stage was.

I needed the controls to stay visible while I worked. I needed color, position, and plain-language reminders I could understand at a glance. I needed something that would help me cross the gap between *this device has so much potential* and *my hands know what to do*.

So I built **Codex Micro Glass Guide**.

It is a translucent Mac guide that floats above my other apps while I learn the device. I can move it, resize it like a normal window, and click any teaching tile for a simple explanation without leaving my work.

The real layout stays visible: the white dial, joystick, black touch sensor, three layer lights, command buttons, and six glowing Agent keys. A quick touch moves through the six programmable layers. The light pattern shows which layer is active. The labels can match the shortcuts I actually use.

I think of it as training wheels made of glass.

The guide stays beside me while repetition becomes familiarity. The point is not to read it forever. The point is to use the hardware often enough that I no longer need it.

## Why it looks a little excessive

I could have made a plain gray reference window. I did not.

The Codex Micro is a tiny, joyful piece of hardware, so its learning guide should feel joyful too. The app uses translucent glass, beveled 3-D controls, soft color, and backlit glows that echo the device itself.

The polish is not only decoration. Color, shape, and position make the controls easier to recognize quickly. The guide becomes a visual memory map instead of another page of instructions.

## A skill and a real Mac app

The public project contains two connected pieces.

The **Codex skill** teaches Codex how to recover a layout, protect private task information, build the guide, and verify the rendered result.

The **generated Mac app** is the movable, resizable overlay you actually use. It can launch offline and stay available while you work. Live destinations inside Codex may still require connectivity, but the learning guide itself does not.

The public repository uses privacy-safe sample tasks. Personal task names, links, and identifiers belong only in your local copy.

## Please do not leave it exactly like mine

This is not one perfect layout for everyone. It is a starting point.

Rename the layers. Change the colors. Rewrite the notes. Match the labels to your real shortcuts. Adapt it for your hands, your brain, your workflow, and your desk.

Then share the privacy-safe ideas that could help someone else: clearer labels, better color systems, accessibility improvements, new teaching patterns, or simply a theme that makes the device more fun to use.

That is the part I am most excited about. A limited-release device does not need to have a limited imagination around it.
