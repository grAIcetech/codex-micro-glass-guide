# HeyGen narration: Codex Micro Glass Guide

I have a very cute—and not inexpensive—device on my desk: the Work Louder and OpenAI Codex Micro.

It was OpenAI’s first branded hardware launch: a functional physical Codex product released with Work Louder in limited quantities.

The demonstration videos make it look almost magical. People fly through their work with a dial, joystick, command keys, six glowing Agent keys, and six programmable layers. I think, yes, I am going to use this for everything.

Then I sit down in front of mine and forget what the buttons do.

That is the less glamorous part of programmable hardware. Seeing what it can do is not the same as remembering how to do it. One button can mean something completely different on another layer. If I have to hunt through documentation and rebuild the map in my head every time, the friction wins.

And I did not buy a lovely, expensive tool so it could become a dust-gathering paperweight.

I want this thing to earn its place on my desk. I want to reach for it every day and let the controls settle into muscle memory. To get there, I needed a bridge between “look at all these possibilities” and “my hands know what to do.”

I am a visual learner, so I built that bridge: Codex Micro Glass Guide.

It is a translucent Mac overlay that floats above my apps while I learn. I can move it, resize it, and click a control for a plain-language reminder without leaving my work.

The dial, joystick, layer lights, touch sensor, command buttons, and six glowing Agent keys live together in one friendly view. Color, position, and depth give my brain landmarks. The playfulness has a job: it helps me recognize controls faster.

The guide also handles the parts that are easy to misread. That glossy black circle is a touch sensor, not a fingerprint scanner. A quick tap cycles through the six programmable layers. Three small lights show which layer is active. The dial, joystick, command keys, and layers can be labeled to match the actions in your own setup.

I think of it as training wheels made of glass. It stays beside me while I need it, quietly translating the hardware until repetition becomes familiarity. Once my hands know the way, I can close it. The guide is not the destination; confidently using the device is.

The project has two pieces. The Codex skill teaches Codex how to recover a layout, protect private task information, generate the guide, and check the real rendered window. The generated Mac app is the movable, resizable overlay that sits on your desktop.

The public project is deliberately generic. It contains privacy-safe sample tasks, while real task names, IDs, and links remain only in your local copy.

And I hope nobody treats my layout as the one correct answer. Rename the layers. Match the labels to the shortcuts you actually use. Change the colors. Rewrite the teaching notes in words that make sense to you. Adapt it for your hands, your brain, your workflow, and your desk.

Then, if you create a theme, a clearer label, or an accessibility improvement that could help someone else, share the privacy-safe version with the community. Keep personal task details local, but let the good ideas travel.

I built Codex Micro Glass Guide because useful tools should help us through the awkward learning stage, not punish us for having one. My goal is not to admire this little device. My goal is to use it until the controls feel natural—and make sure it never has time to gather dust.
